# Module 1 Reading Takeaways

Notes from the two Week 1 Harvard Business Publishing readings:

- Robert J. Dolan, *Framework for Marketing Strategy Formation*
- Sunil Gupta, *Creating Customer Value*

The two readings fit together. Dolan’s framework is the **process**: analyze the 5 Cs, set aspirations (STP), then execute the 4 Ps. Gupta’s reading is the **substance**: what “value” actually is, and why customers pay.

---

## 1. Framework for Marketing Strategy Formation (Dolan)

### Purpose of marketing

Drucker: create a customer. Levitt: create **and keep** a customer through differentiation. Dolan adds a third: turn customers into **brand ambassadors**. Acquisition, retention, and advocacy often need different marketing actions. Winning a customer does not automatically keep them; relationships tend toward entropy unless you fight it.

A marketing strategy is the **blueprint for how the firm plans to compete**. You do not need a mass-market product everyone loves. Focus is the point (Peloton’s ~$2,000 bike, Vizio vs. Bang & Olufsen).

### The process: Analysis → Decisions → Outcomes

Three linked pieces:

1. **Analysis:** the **5 Cs** (customer in the center)
2. **Decisions:** **aspiration (STP)** then **action plan (4 Ps)**
3. **Outcomes:** short-term results **and** a franchise (brand reputation, loyalty)

It is not linear. Analysis and decisions go back and forth.

Product, promotion, and place **create** value. Price **captures** some of that value. The value created by the first three Ps is the **upper bound** on price.

### The 5 Cs

- **Customer:** Who is in the **decision-making unit (DMU)** and what is the **decision-making process (DMP)**? Goal is **product/market fit**. Bonoma’s six DMU roles: initiator, gatekeeper, decider, influencer, purchaser, user. In B2B, ~7 people often fill those six roles.
- **Company:** Strengths, assets, and **core competency** (Prahalad & Hamel): it must contribute to perceived customer value **and** be hard to imitate. Peloton was not “a bike company”; it was trying to revolutionize fitness through technology.
- **Collaborators:** External assets that fill holes (Kickstarter, VCs, manufacturing partners). Peloton kept sales in-house and used partners for the bike and tablet.
- **Competition:** Create more **value (benefits − costs)** than any known alternative. Define competition **broadly**. Peloton’s biggest competitor was often “not working out,” not SoulCycle. Eight of 10 Peloton buyers were not shopping for fitness equipment.
- **Context:** Culture, technology, regulation, law, social norms. Context always changes; products get meaning (and economic value) from culture.

### Aspiration decision: STP

Levitt: “If you’re not thinking segments, you’re not thinking.” Targeting is critical because **customers set the purchase criteria**, so they set the rules of the game.

**Segmentation bases:** demographic, geographic, psychographic/lifestyle, benefit sought, usage. Zipcar mixes several (under 25, urban, environmentally concerned).

A good **positioning statement** specifies:

1. Target customer
2. That customer’s wants
3. Product type/category as the customer sees it
4. Key benefit

Anderson, Narus, and van Rossum: use a **resonating focus** on the one or two points of difference that deliver the greatest value. Kotler: solving positioning lets you solve the marketing mix; the 4 Ps are the tactics of positioning.

Meaningful targeting **excludes** some customers (early Dell, Ross MBA). The goal is a segment you can reach efficiently, that values your advantage, and that will pay for it.

### Action plan: 4 Ps

**Product** is the full bundle of benefits, not the core function (Levitt’s **total product**). Nest vs. a $25 thermostat; Amex Gold/Platinum vs. a basic charge card.

Three product-policy decisions:

- **Breadth:** how many product lines (BMW vs. LVMH)
- **Depth / line architecture:** good-better-best, different segments, or same price/different tastes
- **Evolution:** new-and-improved, trade up (Tanqueray No. Ten), trade down (Delta Basic Economy)

Cooper’s new-product success driver: a **differentiated product with unique benefits and a compelling value proposition**, built with voice of the customer and spiral development (concept → prototype → feedback → revise).

**Promotion** now includes the **Zero Moment of Truth** (Google): customers search and talk about the brand before you control the message. You need an **integrated communications plan**.

**6 Ms:** Market, Mission, Message, Media, Money, Measurement.

Communication vehicles vary on two axes: **one-way vs. two-way**, and **mass vs. addressable/customized**. Personal selling is expensive because it can customize the message.

**Push vs. pull:** push incentivizes intermediaries; pull creates customer preference so retailers mainly make the product available.

**Place** must handle four channel tasks: generate demand, fulfill demand, after-sales service, and feedback. Design choice: direct/DIY vs. partners. Then **channel management** (who has power, how conflict is handled). Omnichannel is common; partners claim a real share of revenue (Best Buy ~23%).

**Price** is how the firm captures value. A 1% improvement in price realization is ~12% to the Fortune 500 bottom line.

Price between **COGS (lower bound)** and **perceived value (upper bound)**. Sometimes sell below cost to seed trials.

**True economic value (TEV)** is always relative to the customer’s **next-best alternative**.

Three pricing pillars:

1. Value to the customer, not cost, should drive price
2. Customize price when value differs across customers
3. Understand price sensitivity

**Price customization types:** buyer characteristics, transaction characteristics, product-line variations (Disney uses all three).

Price sensitivity **rises** when: the end user pays; the item is a large share of spend; the buyer resells into a competitive market; quality can be judged without using price as a signal; shopping around is easy; there is no urgency; switching costs are low.

### Mix quality

Shapiro’s three degrees of mix interaction: **consistency** (minimum), **integration** (positive interaction), **leverage** (each P used to its best advantage).

## 2. Creating Customer Value (Gupta)

Value = **what the customer gets minus what they pay**. Firms can be **market-driven** (research first, e.g. Nabisco 100-calorie packs) or **market-driving** (vision first, e.g. Jobs/Apple). Either way, you have to know what people will pay for.

Four types of value:

### Economic value (EVC)

Tangible monetary savings at purchase or over the life of the product. Think **total cost of ownership / life-cycle cost**, not sticker price (LED vs. incandescent; SKF bearings; hybrid cars).

**EVC** = maximum price a customer *should* be willing to pay versus the current/reference product. Firms usually charge **less than EVC** to give a switching incentive. In Gupta’s machine example: EVC $600, cost $300, price $475; customer still saves $125 even though the sticker is higher.

Three rules:

1. EVC is a **ceiling**, not the actual price
2. Compare over the **same time horizon**
3. EVC is **relative** — it changes with the customer’s current product and usage (SuperOil is worth more to high-mileage drivers)

**Marketing uses:** pricing, segmentation/targeting, and positioning of new products. Economic benefit alone often is not enough; customers may not believe the claims.

### Functional value

Features and performance that are hard to convert into a single dollar number (phones, laptops).

**Multi-attribute model:**

Preference for brand *j* = Σ (importance of attribute *i*) × (rating of brand *j* on *i*)

Levers if you lose: improve weak attributes, attack competitors’ ratings, change which attributes customers think matter, target a segment that values your strengths, or introduce a **new** attribute (coffee percolator with dechlorinator + grinder).

Limits:

- It is **compensatory**; real buyers often use **noncompensatory** rules (conjunctive: must clear a threshold on every attribute)
- People cannot easily articulate weights → **conjoint analysis**

**Features ≠ benefits.** Levitt/McGinneva: customers buy a **quarter-inch hole**, not a quarter-inch drill. Compete on the job to be done, or you get a **features war** (Gillette vs. Schick blades) and **feature fatigue** (more features raise perceived capability but lower usability; after use, usability matters more). Apple/Philips/Mercedes cut features on purpose.

Economic and functional value are tangible and **easier for competitors to copy**.

### Experiential value (harder to imitate)

Four sources: **branding, design, customer experience/service, emotional benefits**.

- **Branding:** quality/status/image; premiums often exceed quality cost (Lexus vs. Camry; New Coke vs. Classic).
- **Design:** product, packaging, stores (Apple; Mayo Clinic lobby).
- **Service:** 80% of leaders think they deliver superior service; customers say **8%** do. **Service-profit chain:** internal quality → employee satisfaction → customer satisfaction → loyalty → profit. Hire front-line for **attitude**, not just aptitude (Southwest); treat them well (Starbucks “partners”). Simpler product lines help service (Commerce Bank). If you offload work to customers, **simplify the task**.
- **Emotional benefits:** P&G “Thank You, Mom” — not product features, an emotional bond.

### Social value

Value from other people, not just the product itself.

- **Network effects:** value rises as more people adopt (Facebook, WhatsApp, YouTube). Speed to scale and shareability matter.
- **Preference formation:** preferences are often **constructed** and socially influenced (herding in music downloads; up-votes snowball). Netflix/Amazon recommendations exist because preferences are weak.
- **Social capital:** people create UGC from reciprocity, empowerment, and status (Yelp Elite, followers).
- **Social relationships:** Rivigo solved a **social** truck-driver problem (time at home, status), not a wage problem. Malls shifting from transactions to social/entertainment.

## How they connect for Week 1

Dolan’s framework is **how you choose customers and compete**. Gupta’s four types of value are **what you are competing with**. Peloton works in both: 5 Cs + STP + mix, and value that is economic (vs. studio memberships over time), functional (smart bike + content), experiential (instructors, music, community), and social (the peloton / online tribe).

Highest-yield pieces for class and the quiz: **5 Cs, STP, 4 Ps as create vs. capture, EVC vs. price, features vs. benefits, and why experiential/social value is harder to copy.**
