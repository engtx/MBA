# Forge Bikes — Quarter 1 Strategy Brief

Working brief for the Marketplace simulation (BUS 655). Q1 decisions locked in this document:

| Decision | Choice | One-line why |
|---|---|---|
| Company name | **Forge Bikes** | Crafted / durable / custom. Matches Mountain, not Rec or Speed. |

## 1. What Q1 is actually asking

The Help File’s Q1 theme is: *what path are you going to take initially?* You name the firm, profile the segments, pick **one** target, and schedule **one** outlet. The outlet takes a full quarter to open, so Q1 is the test-market location for Q2.

You are not trying to cover the world. Class and the Help File both say the same thing: pick a segment you can serve, that values what 3D carbon fiber actually does, and that one store can reach. Large is not the same as attractive.

Headquarters grades you later on a balanced scorecard, not on Q1 volume:

```
Total Performance = (Financial + Market Performance + Marketing Effectiveness) / 3
```

- **Financial:** net profit vs. a $4,000,000 HQ expectation. You will lose money in the test-market quarters. That is normal.
- **Market performance:** share in your *primary and secondary* target segments (secondary is a Q4 decision).
- **Marketing effectiveness:** brand judgment + ad judgment in those same two segments.

That last piece is why the target choice matters more than city size. If most of your sales land in a segment you did not name, the marketing score drops even if revenue looks fine.

You can sell to people outside the target. Spillover still helps profit. The scorecard just wants the *majority* of sales in the segment you claimed.

## 2. Segment profiles (do this before you defend the pick)

Help File instruction: form a mental picture of each segment, then give it a name. Ratings are standardized to a mean of 100.

- **110+** = this need distinguishes the segment. You must deliver it.
- **100–110** = table stakes. Fine, not distinctive.
- **Below 100** = can *hurt* value if you over-build it. The scientific-calculator example in the Help File is about this.

### Recreation — “Weekend Path Riders”

Fun and a little exercise with friends and family. Short rides, bike paths, light terrain, maybe a picnic. Moderately price sensitive. Will pay extra for comfort and ease of use, **not** for performance.

| Top needs | Score | Applications | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy to ride | 129 | Recreation | 129 |
| Comfortable | 123 | Fun | 123 |
| Feel young at heart | 123 | Relaxation | 122 |
| Safety | 122 | Exercise | 115 |
| Simple to use | 121 | Adventure | 112 |
| Low price point | 118 | Sport | **81** |

Price they will consider for the *ideal* brand: **$900** (treat as a ceiling).

Carbon fiber’s light weight and vibration damping help them. Custom fit and “elite bike” status help a bit (status 114). But this segment punishes complexity. A fully loaded mountain or aero bike can look like that 36-function calculator: more capability, less desire. Sport at 81 is a warning label.

### Mountain — “Trail Adventurers” (our target)

Off-road. Adventure, exercise, sport. Hills, descents, cross-country. Pride in skill and an adrenaline rush. Will pay a **premium** for features that get them across difficult terrain. They want sturdy, powerful, agile.

| Top needs | Score | Applications | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Durable | 129 | Adventure | 129 |
| Can handle rough terrain | 127 | Sport | 120 |
| Ability to turn sharply | 123 | Exercise | 118 |
| Soften impact of rough terrain | 121 | Recreation / Fun | 115 |
| Can stop quickly | 120 | Relaxation | 108 |
| Light weight | 119 | | |
| Stylish + status / exclusivity | 118 | | |
| Navigation assistance | 116 | | |

**Landmines (below 100):** sleek **85**, aerodynamic **89**, low price **98**, easy to ride **97**. Do not design a road-racer that happens to have knobby tires.

Price ceiling for the ideal brand: **$1,120**.

### Speed — “Aero Racers”

Lightest, fastest, most aerodynamic bike. Long distance, races, group rides. Will pay a high price and give up comfort and utility for an edge.

| Top needs | Score | Applications | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speed | 129 | Sport | 129 |
| Light weight | 128 | Exercise | 121 |
| Aerodynamic | 124 | Adventure | 117 |
| Durable | 122 | Recreation / Fun / Relax | ~102–106 |
| Navigation | 122 | | |
| Status / exclusivity | 121 | | |

**Landmines:** low price **69**, simple to use **71**, able to carry things **71**, rough terrain **78**. Price ceiling: **$1,300**.

## 3. Why Mountain, not Recreation or Speed

Week 2 targeting scorecard: segment attractiveness + company fit + competition. Gupta’s version of the same test.

### Segment attractiveness

| | Recreation | Mountain | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12-month units (all cities) | 23,536 | 15,555 | 14,374 |
| Price ceiling | $900 | $1,120 | $1,300 |
| Global $ potential (price × units) | $21.2M | $17.4M | $18.7M |
| Price sensitivity | High (low-price need = 118) | Low (98) | Very low (69) |
| Wants carbon-fiber *performance* | Weakly | Strongly | Strongly |

Recreation is the volume segment. Speed is the margin segment. Mountain is the middle circle on the sim’s own price/performance chart — and that is the point.

Help File: multiply price willing to pay × potential demand, then pick targets and cities from that. Recreation wins on raw dollars. That is also why classmates will pile into it.

### Company fit (this is the real reason)

The product is a **3D-printed, custom carbon-fiber** bike: lighter, vibration-damping, custom to body and terrain, cheaper than today’s elite carbon, still a stretch vs. metal. The Game Scenario says today’s carbon sales are already “limited to high performance **road and mountain** bikes by elite users.” You are bringing that product down into the high end of the conventional market.

Mountain’s top five needs are almost a spec sheet for that technology:

1. Durable → carbon fiber, rugged frame
2. Rough terrain → mountain tires, geometry, gears, suspension
3. Sharp turning → agile frame, power bars
4. Soften impact → carbon damping + full suspension
5. Stop quickly → disc brakes

Recreation wants easy / comfortable / cheap / simple. You *can* build that, but you leave the distinctive capability on the table and you compete on price in the biggest, most price-sensitive pool. Speed wants aero and a $1,300 sticker. Also a fair fit technically — worse fit with the Forge identity, and NYC/Amsterdam Speed will be a knife fight.

### Competitive room

Everyone starts equal. Nobody has a brand yet. The predictable first moves:

- **Recreation + Amsterdam** — largest single cell in the survey (6,830 units).
- **Speed + New York or Amsterdam** — highest willingness to pay, big Speed pools.

Mountain is the segment people skip if they only sort the table by “biggest” or “richest.” That is a feature. The Help File’s how-to-win chapter is explicit: find the group whose needs are not well served.

### Positioning (use this in the reflection)

External (class template):

> Among trail riders who want adventure on difficult terrain, Forge Bikes is the custom carbon-fiber mountain bike that is sturdy, agile, and built to their body, because 3D printing delivers durability, traction, and impact control without the elite carbon price.

Internal:

> To Mountain buyers, Forge is the trail bike that gets you down the hard line — not a comfort cruiser and not a road rocket.

Value types (Gupta), so the 4 Ps have a spine in Q2:

| Type | What Mountain is buying |
|---|---|
| Functional | Durability, terrain, turning, braking, low weight, impact control |
| Economic | Elite-carbon capability at a conventional high-end price (~$1,120 ceiling) |
| Experiential | Custom fit, the ride, the adrenaline, the shop experience |
| Social | Status / exclusivity (118), stylish (118), “I ride the real thing” |

Functional and economic are copyable. Experiential and social (Forge, custom, the anvil, the fit process) are the harder-to-copy layer. Lean on those in ads even though the product has to be functionally right first.

## 4. Why Rio, not New York

Help File territory rule: pick cities with the **highest concentration** of your target, then balance size against operating cost and expected crowding. “Better” markets cost more to occupy and attract more firms.

### The survey, rewritten as money and mix

12-month potential units:

| City | Recreation | Mountain | Speed | Total | Mountain share of city |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| New York | 5,836 | **4,962** | 4,833 | 15,631 | 31.7% |
| **Rio** | 4,978 | **3,771** | 2,304 | 11,053 | **34.1%** |
| Amsterdam | 6,830 | 3,428 | 4,415 | 14,673 | 23.4% |
| Bangalore | 5,892 | 3,394 | 2,822 | 12,108 | 28.0% |

Mountain dollar potential (units × $1,120):

| City | Mountain $ potential | Rank |
|---|---|---|
| New York | $5.56M | 1 |
| **Rio** | **$4.22M** | **2** |
| Amsterdam | $3.84M | 3 |
| Bangalore | $3.80M | 4 |

Rio is **not** the biggest Mountain city. New York is. Rio is the **most Mountain city** — 34% of local demand vs. 32% in NYC, 28% in Bangalore, 23% in Amsterdam. That is the Help File’s concentration test, not a vibe.

### Why concentration beats raw size in Q1

1. **One store, one brand, one ad, ≤7 salespeople (Q2 cap).** You cannot harvest NYC’s extra ~1,200 Mountain units in the test quarter anyway. Actual demand will be far below potential until the category is taught.
2. **Cost.** Help File: the better markets have higher setup, lease, and salary costs. NYC is the richest total market (~$17.1M across segments). You will lose money in Q2–Q3 regardless. A cheaper city is a better classroom.
3. **Crowding.** NYC is #1 Mountain *and* #1 Speed. Amsterdam is #1 Recreation and #2 Speed. Those two cities are where volume-seekers and margin-seekers both land. Rio’s Speed pool is the smallest of the four (2,304). People chasing Speed will not open here first.
4. **Pioneering.** Early entry builds loyalty; late entry pays a tax to unwind it. If Rio is thin in Q2, Forge owns the local story before the category gets noisy.
5. **Spillover without losing the plot.** Rio still has 4,978 Recreation units. A Mountain bike that is not a horror show on a path can pick up some of those. They help cash. They should not become the ad.

### The honest risk

If several teams run the same concentration math, Rio gets crowded and the size disadvantage shows. If *nobody* else opens Rio, category awareness stays low and the market develops slowly. Watch Q3 competitor maps. The planned second Mountain city is **New York** (see §7), not a second South American bet.

Do not open Amsterdam as a Mountain store until you have a Recreation (or Speed) brand. Mountain is only 23% of that city.

## 5. Why the name Forge Bikes works

Help File on company name: pick the image first. The name sets expectations. Behavior finishes the job. Product brand names later **do not change demand** in the sim; they still matter for the reflection and for keeping a product line readable (Titan → Titan II).

Forge + anvil + hammers + “Custom Bicycles” says:

- **Made**, not merchandised. Fits 3D print / made-to-order / no inventory.
- **Strong.** Mountain’s #1 need is durable.
- **Custom.** The actual business model, and the status story (118).
- **Not sleek, not family-picnic, not aero.** The logo would lie if you had picked Recreation or Speed.

Keep the industrial / trail register in Q2 ads. Do not suddenly sound like a café-racer or a recumbent comfort brand.

Suggested first product name direction (Q2, demand-neutral but useful): something that stays in the Forge family and says terrain — e.g. **Forge Trail**, **Forge Ridge**, **Anvil**. When you revise after test market, keep ≥60% of the name (`Forge Trail` → `Forge Trail 2` / `Forge Trail XT`) so loyalty can transfer.

## 7. Path after Q1 (so the first three choices stay coherent)

Strategic direction, tentative through Q3, firm by Q4 (Help File).

**Primary:** Mountain, everywhere we open.  
**Secondary (Q4):** Recreation *if* the test bike is not too intimidating and Rio/NYC Rec demand is under-served; Speed *if* the Mountain bike already scores well on light weight and you can add an aero sibling without wrecking the Forge story. Do not pick the secondary until Q3 results are in. The scorecard will punish a secondary you do not actually win.

**City sequence (Mountain lens):**

| Quarter | Outlet action | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 (schedule) / Q2 (open) | **Rio** | Concentration, cost, less crowding |
| Q2 schedule / Q3 open | **New York** (optional but likely) | Largest Mountain $ pool; go once the brand/price/ad are less wrong |
| Q4 | Bangalore or a second Rio-area bet only if NYC is a bloodbath | Bangalore Mountain $ ≈ Amsterdam, less Rec/Speed gravity |
| Later | Amsterdam | Only with a Rec or Speed brand on the floor |

Q2 caps: 1 brand, 1 ad, $20,000 ad spend, 7 people in the store, price $400–$2,100. You may schedule a second outlet.

### Q2 brand: match features to Mountain benefits

Start from benefits, then pick components (Help File QFD). Do not max every spec — materials × 2 or 3 is the rough retail, and **$1,120 is the ceiling**. Over-building forces a sticker that empties the funnel.

| Mountain need | Likely component (from Help File glossary) | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Rough terrain + durability | **Rugged agile frame**; standard or enriched carbon | Relaxed comfort frame; sleek aero frame |
| Traction | **High-grip or super-traction mountain tires** | Racing tires; maybe even hybrids |
| Stop quickly | **Standard disc brakes** | Basic rim brakes |
| Sharp turning / power over obstacles | **Power straight bars** (or carbon riser if vibration + comfort show up in tests) | Drop / aero bars |
| Varied terrain, climbs | **24-speed (3×8)** | 7-speed city gearing; 14-speed “not for tough mountain climbs” |
| Soften impact | **Full suspension** (front + rear) | No suspension; front-only if you must cut cost |
| Light weight | Enriched carbon **if it still prices under ~$1,120** | Electric motor on v1 (range/hills help, weight and cost hurt) |
| Navigation (116) | **Performance bike computer** | — |
| Stylish / status (118) | Segment-styled **bright decals** | Thin “classy brushstroke” (reads Speed/luxe, not trail) |
| Carry things (104) | Skip | Baskets |
| Sleek / aero (85 / 89) | Skip | Aero tubes, racing seat, drop bars |

Seat: all-purpose polymer gel, not the racing saddle.

**Price.** Set at or **under** $1,120 until you have a price judgment. Help File: above the threshold, people never enter the market. All segments like lower prices; Mountain is less elastic than Rec, so do not race to the bottom. **Skip rebates** on Mountain. The Help File says rebates can *worsen* price judgment for inelastic / value-driven buyers (looks like you are dumping inventory). Rec later: $50–$100 rebates are the tool.

**Sales force (Q2).** Cap is 7. Help File starting guess: ~50 units per salesperson per quarter (range 30–70). Specialists spend 70% on their segment. Unassigned people are less efficient. Put most of the seven on **Mountain specialty**. Add **one service** person — after-sale support (flats, tune-ups) lifts segments that care about service, and Mountain bikes get beaten up. Do not staff as if Rio will produce Q6 demand.

**Ads (Q2).** One design, $20k max. Talk benefits (adventure, terrain, custom fit, durable), not carbon-fiber trivia. Fast-test ad scores in the 70s are already hard; 80+ is very good. You will rewrite this after Q3.

**Q3 discipline.** The first brand will be wrong in some way. Apple pulled Lisa in seven months — the Help File uses that on purpose. Fix from brand / price / ad *judgments*, not from ego. If you change the brand after Q2, **change the name**.

## 8. Reflection / “justify your decisions” language

The Help File says be ready to justify Q1. A tight version:

> We named the company Forge Bikes to signal custom-built strength, which is the story 3D carbon fiber can actually tell. We targeted Mountain rather than Recreation because the product’s distinctive benefits — durability, terrain capability, impact control, low weight, custom fit — are Mountain’s highest-rated needs. Recreation is larger and more price-sensitive; winning it would require a simpler, cheaper bike and would put us in the most obvious competitive set. Speed fits the technology but fights over New York and Amsterdam and does not match the Forge identity.
>
> We opened in Rio de Janeiro because, of the four test cities, it has the highest concentration of Mountain demand (34% of local potential) and the second-highest Mountain dollar potential. New York is larger in absolute Mountain units, but it is also the strongest Speed market and will carry higher operating cost and more rivals. Q1 is a test market with one store. Concentration, cost, and expected crowding matter more than winning the biggest cell on the page.
