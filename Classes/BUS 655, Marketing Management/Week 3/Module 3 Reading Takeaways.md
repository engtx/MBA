Notes from the two Week 3 Harvard readings:

Week 3 is the **Customer C** in depth. Week 1 said understand the DMU and DMP. These readings are how people (and organizations) actually decide. Same skeleton in B2C and B2B: a process, a unit of people, and a need you have to trigger and then fulfill. The mechanics differ.

## How the two readings fit together

| | B2C (Gourville & Norton) | B2B (Cespedes & Narayandas) |
|---|---|---|
| Who buys | Many customers, often one decision-maker | Fewer customers, an **organizational buying center (OBC)** |
| What is bought | Mass-produced, fixed price | Often customized, negotiated price, larger tickets |
| Demand | Media can stimulate it | **Derived** from downstream / end-consumer demand |

For Broadcom: you sell B2B (Apple’s OBC) but Apple’s demand is derived from consumers. Both readings apply.

## 1. Consumer Behavior and the Buying Process

Levitt again: people buy the hole, not the drill. Firms that skip this fail in public (Segway, dollar coin, Webvan). Behavior is not random. It is driven by **the product, the context, and the people**.

### Four ways decisions vary

These are lenses, not either/or. A purchase can be cognitive *and* high-involvement *and* noncompensatory.

**1. Cognitive vs. emotional**

- Cognitive: slow, systematic, information (insurance, *Consumer Reports*). Utilitarian products and **search goods** (you can research almost everything before buying).
- Emotional: faster, idiosyncratic, “I’ll know it when I see it.” Hedonic / ego-expressive products and **experience goods** (you only know after you use them).
- Same product can flip with **context** (pickup as a job tool vs. a rugged-identity statement) and **person** (teen iPod silhouette ads vs. Verizon “Can you hear me now”).
- Marketing implication: cognitive buyers will hunt you down — facts, features, comparison. Emotional buyers need visibility and impulse placement (homepage, end-cap) plus imagery.

**2. High- vs. low-involvement**

- High: effortful, long, fully engaged (house, car, wedding dress). Can be cognitive *or* emotional.
- Low: almost mindless (extend the gym membership).
- Involvement **changes over time** (first car insurance vs. the renewal) and **by person** (wine geek vs. whoever is on sale).
- High-involvement: information, sales help, risk reducers. Low-involvement: make it easy, stand out, reminder cues, maybe invent a difference (“flavor crystals”).

**3. Optimize vs. satisfice** (Herbert Simon)

- Optimize = hunt for the best. Satisfice = “good enough”; more search is not worth it.
- More likely to optimize when expense is high, quality/price variance is high, or you will live with it a long time (buy a vacation home vs. rent one week; pick a university vs. a week-long class).
- Some people optimize sandwiches; some satisfice houses. Both can be dumb.
- Satisficers: easy to find, big facings, cheap enough. Optimizers: advertise superiority, raise the cost of a bad choice, price as a quality signal.

**4. Compensatory vs. noncompensatory**

- Compensatory: weaknesses can be offset (early iPhone: bad battery, high price, but usability won). Market the **total package**; put all the info out.
- Noncompensatory: miss one threshold and you are out (must be a nonstop). Do well on the **one or two** attributes that kill the deal.
- Big choice sets → people add noncompensatory filters first. One dominant attribute → noncompensatory is rational (Volvo = safest; ignore MPG).
- You can **push** the process: if you win on one thing, make that the only thing that matters.

This is the same multi-attribute idea from Gupta Week 1 (compensatory model vs. conjunctive threshold).

### The decision-making process (DMP)

Three phases. Fail any one and you lose this sale or the next one.

**Pre-purchase:** trigger (cold → warm/hot) → search → total set → **awareness set → consideration set → choice set → decision**.

- Marketers can plant the trigger (mattress every 10 years, oil-change sticker, jewelry before Valentine’s).
- You have to get into awareness, then consideration, then choice. Shelf layout matters (all of one brand together vs. all shampoos together).
- You can change which attributes they use to cut the set (Hyundai warranties; IKEA “furniture doesn’t have to last forever”).

**Purchase:** brand, seller, quantity, timing, payment. Intent ≠ sale. Barriers: too many sofa fabrics, a salesperson steers them, credit fails. Remove friction. If they only decided “white wine,” the label and the facing still win.

**Post-purchase:** loyalty is not automatic. **Buyer’s remorse** after a big spend. Happy customers repurchase and talk; unhappy ones return, complain, warn others. Manage aftercare, returns, warranties, loaners, the next purchase (fading toothbrush bristles, subscription renewals), and **disposal** (old mattress, battery, HP cartridges). That last piece is how you keep them.

Shopper types (habitual, deal-seekers, variety-lovers, high-involvement) move through the three phases differently.

### The decision-making unit (DMU)

Always at least the buyer. Often more. Same roles as Dolan Week 1: buyer, influencer, gatekeeper, approver, user (kids on cereal). Market to **every role**, not just the wallet. Cereal: vitamins for mom, cartoon + kid-height shelf for the child.

### Propecia (the worked example)

Merck had 40 million balding men and two tools: doctor detailing and DTC ads. The DMP was the problem:

- Men underestimate hair loss, delay, try hats/comb-overs first.
- Doctors saw it as cosmetic, remembered weak Rogaine, worried about side effects.
- Drug works better at **keeping** hair than growing it, so delay kills the product.

Two DMUs: physician (aware/skeptical → comfortable → confident → proactive) and patient (denial → disguise → maybe a doctor). For most Merck drugs the doctor leads. For Propecia the **patient had to pull** and the doctor just had to not block. So: urgency ads to get men in early; detail primary-care doctors (first stop) more than dermatologists (friendlier but not the first visit). Match the selling process to the buying process.

### Supplemental (worth knowing for class)

Social media spreads both love and failure. Co-creation pulls customers into design. “Conscience” marketing: values and ethics as part of the buy.

## 2. Business-to-Business Marketing

B2B = sell to businesses, governments, nonprofits. Same STP/value ideas as B2C, different constraints. B2B is huge (majority of e-commerce dollars). Demand is **derived**: more restaurant meals → more ovens → more steel.

Five steps: strategy → select markets → know the buyer → communicate benefits → align the organization.

### Strategy first (and it sticks)

B2B choices about **market, price, distribution, and communication** lock up capital and people for a long time. Hawker Beechcraft targeted only cheap jets, could not climb into luxury/military when that market died, went bankrupt.

Strategy = **objectives, scope, advantage**. Marketing both follows and then traps those.

Four marketing components:

1. **Market selection** is a strategy choice (IBM PureSystems for high-volume transaction buyers). Hard to unwind.
2. **Price** varies by how the product is **used** (buried pipe vs. surface irrigation) and whether the buyer can pass cost down the chain. Lots of customized pricing, little comparison shopping.
3. **Distribution:** direct sales (complex, concentrated, high ticket), distributors (fragmented, small orders), reps/agents (small firms), brokers (big lots). Multichannel is sticky. Partners/SIs bundle.
4. **Communication:** trade shows (often #2 spend after sales force), journals, email, content/white papers, webinars. **Personal selling** is still the workhorse: custom solutions, handle performance issues, watch value over time.

### Selecting markets = three-way fit

**Market–firm–product.** Not just “big segment.”

- Who in the demand chain will pay for the benefits you can actually deliver?
- What product delivers those benefits?
- What is the firm better at than rivals?

Volumes can differ 100x between accounts. You may target for profit, share, or learning — not all three. A weak fit that later collapses (Meyer gift boxes when retailers stopped needing them) is a strategy failure, not a sales miss.

### Knowing the buyer (OBC / DMU)

Markets don’t buy. People in an **organizational buying center** do.

Typical functions and what they care about:

| Function | Cares about |
|---|---|
| Engineering | Specs, qualify vendors, evaluate alternatives |
| Manufacturing | Make vs. buy, delivery, current supplier performance |
| Marketing | Will this help us sell / price the end product? |
| General management | Strategy, relationships, stakeholders |
| Purchasing | Price, vendor performance, coordinate everyone |

Same person can wear several hats on a cheap SaaS deal; enterprise software splits them.

OBCs differ in **size, formalization, centralization**, and **lateral / vertical involvement**. Bigger and more layered when the buy is new, risky, or complex.

Buying is **economic and personal**. The buyer’s “customers” are her boss and peers. Trust in the salesperson matters. Some want a long partnership; some want a one-off transaction.

Build **personas** per role (Oversight: compliance officer vs. T&E manager vs. CFO).

**Buy classes** (risk and OBC size go up as you go down the list):

- **Straight rebuy** — routine, purchasing clerk, low risk
- **Modified rebuy** — change spec or vendor, affected functions jump in
- **New task** — never bought this before, biggest OBC, most risk

**Four buying stages:**

1. Recognize need / benefits sought (including make vs. buy)
2. Set specs and **qualify** sources (you can be out before the RFP if you are not on the certified list)
3. RFP / evaluate proposals
4. Select, negotiate, implement, and later review the vendor

Different OBC members dominate different stages. Technical folks write specs and are less price-sensitive; purchasing runs the RFP and the price fight.

### Benefits, value, price

Benefits (real or perceived) **are** value, and value sets the ceiling on price. Do more than answer the RFP. Narayandas’s four-box:

| | Tangible | Intangible |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | Measurable savings (less spoilage in a better reefer truck). Opens doors; easy to copy unless unique. | Claimed savings the buyer cannot verify yet (energy audit). Need trials, guarantees, pay-for-performance, third-party tests. |
| **Noneconomic** | Reputation, scale, specialized capability. Slow and expensive to build; can hold a premium. | Trust, conscientiousness. Only proven by experience. Rarely wins the first deal; keeps the account (rep who comes back from vacation to fix a crash). |

Rules of thumb:

1. **Win the order** with economic + tangible. Brand can differentiate if you have it.
2. **Win the customer** by matching on economic/tangible, then differentiate with noneconomic/tangible.
3. **Keep the customer** by migrating them toward noneconomic/intangible (the relationship).

Match the benefit to the OBC member (recycling coordinator vs. engineer vs. purchasing vs. mayor’s office). Same machine, four stories.

**Value proposition** (Anderson, Narus, van Rossum — same “resonating focus” as Week 1):

- **All benefits** — laundry list; weak
- **Favorable points of difference** — vs. next-best alternative; better
- **Resonating focus** — the one or two differences that matter most and that you can keep improving; best in B2B

Then document it over time. That is how you defend price.

### Align the organization

Marketing, sales, product, and service have to sell the **same** value proposition to the **same** OBC. Compensation, who owns the account, and who can change the offer all have to match the strategy. If sales is paid on volume and marketing positioned a premium, the mix breaks.

## How this sits on the 5 Cs / STP / 4 Ps

- **Customer C:** DMP + DMU (B2C) or OBC + buy class + stage (B2B).
- **Segmentation:** who / what they have done / **why they buy** — these readings are the “why” and the “how they decide.”
- **Targeting:** in B2B, fit + cost-to-serve + which OBC you can actually persuade. Derived demand means you also target the downstream market your customer serves.
- **Positioning / 4 Ps:** cognitive vs. emotional changes promotion and place. Compensatory vs. noncompensatory changes what you claim. B2B price follows documented usage value, not a sticker. Personal selling is often the promotion P.

Propecia and Broadcom are the same movie: map every person in the unit, map their process, then design the mix so nobody in that unit can kill the deal.
