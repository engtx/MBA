## 1. Week 1 recap (slides 3–8, 25–32)

Marketing (AMA): creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society.

**Customer value takeaways from class:**

- Value is why customers buy **and** why they stay loyal.
- Forms of value overlap and reinforce each other (economic, functional, experiential, social).
- The marketer’s job is to maximize the **salient** forms of value.
- Providing value is a whole-company effort, not a department-only job.

Then the deck restates Dolan’s framework:

1. **Analysis (5 Cs)** — research and ongoing monitoring; customers in the center.
2. **Aspiration (STP)** — what value to whom.
3. **Action plan (4 Ps)** — product, promotion, and place **create** value; price **captures** it.
4. **Outcomes** — acquire, retain, and turn customers into ambassadors; plus firm performance.

**5 Cs as class framed them:**

- **Customers:** who they are, how they decide, the journey (pre / during / post), is the market big enough?
- **Company:** can we create and deliver value in a way that is sustainable and differentiable? New products/markets must match the skill set.
- **Collaborators:** vertical (supply chain, channels) and horizontal partnerships; in-house vs. outsource; financial viability.
- **Competition:** customers judge value **relative to other options**; current and future rivals; offerings, strengths/weaknesses, barriers to entry.
- **Context:** technology (including disruption from competitors), culture, politics/regulation/law, natural environment.

## 2. Competitive strategy (slides 33–40)

Why study competition in marketing: customer perceptions of you vs. rivals **shape positioning**. Differentiation has to be meaningful. Who counts as a competitor depends on your goals. The work is ongoing, not a one-time scan.

**Levitt, “Differentiation of Anything”:**

- A product is a bundle of attributes / a “cluster of value satisfactions.”
- Value = ability to **solve the customer’s problem** (same idea as Gupta’s drill/hole).
- Price competition is unsustainable.
- There is no such thing as a commodity; everything can be differentiated.

**A competitor is any organization that aims to satisfy the same customer need.** Four current levels, plus potential rivals and substitutes:

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Brand | Same product form, other brands (Coke vs. Pepsi) |
| Product category | Same category, different form |
| Generic | Different products that solve the same need (Peloton vs. “not working out”) |
| Total budget | Anything competing for the same spend |

**Perceptual maps** plot brands on the attributes customers actually use (class example: soap on moisturizing vs. deodorant). They show clusters, gaps, and whether you occupy a distinct place. That is positioning made visual.

This is Dolan’s “define competition broadly” with a usable taxonomy.

## 3. Segmentation (slides 41–61)

**Mass marketing / full market coverage** (the alternative to STP): no segments, one offer for everyone. Efficient to produce. Works only if needs are truly homogeneous. Fewer messages, but often expensive, and you are easy to attack. Unless you choose to be a mass marketer, segment.

**What segmentation is:** split a heterogeneous market into homogeneous subgroups with similar needs. You **discover** segments; you do not create them. Payoff: tailored offers, clearer opportunities, products that actually solve the problem instead of half-solving it.

**Why bother (Gupta + class):**

- For customers: convenience, tailored products, relevant messages, better experience.
- For the firm: find unmet needs, design better offers, spend more efficiently, grow profit.

**Variables (same list as Dolan/Gupta):**

- Demographic, geographic, behavioral/usage, psychographic, benefits sought.

Class extra on **B2B**: geographic and firmographics are easy (and easy for rivals to copy). Buying process, volume/loyalty, and benefits sought are harder to do and harder to imitate.

**A viable segment (B2B or B2C) must be:**

1. **Identifiable** — you can find and measure the people in it
2. **Substantial** — large enough to serve profitably; watch cannibalization
3. **Accessible** — you can reach it with comms and distribution, apart from other segments
4. **Stable** — lasts long enough for marketing to work
5. **Differentiable** — similar inside the segment, different from other segments
6. **Actionable** — you can actually build an offer and tactics for it

**Segment profiles** answer three questions (this is the spine of the Gupta reading):

1. Who are the customers?
2. What is their behavior (usage, loyalty, profitability)?
3. Why do they buy (needs, decision process, value)?

Q1 of the simulation is basically: read the market survey, build this kind of profile, pick one segment.

## 4. Targeting (slides 62–66)

Targeting = evaluate attractiveness → pick one or more segments → design the mix for them. Goal: **long-term profitability** in the chosen segments, not coverage for its own sake.

**Score a segment on three buckets** (matches Gupta §2.3):

| Segment | Organizational | Competitive |
|---|---|---|
| Size and value | Competencies and objectives | Intensity of rivalry |
| Forecast growth | Ability to reach them | How satisfied they are now |
| Spend now and later | Cost to provide the value | Competitor resources |
| Profit (sales − cost to serve) | | Barriers to entry |

**Approaches:**

- **Single target market** — one segment, one mix (focus).
- **Multiple target market / segmenter** — different mix per segment.
- **Combiner** — two or more submarkets with the **same** mix (cheaper, weaker fit).

Week 1 already said meaningful targeting **excludes** some customers. These slides are the how.

## 5. Positioning (slides 67–73)

Positioning is the mental image you want relative to competitors. If you do not manage it, customers will position you themselves. The statement should align the whole company, not just ads.

**Templates from class:**

- External: *Among [target], [brand] is the [frame of reference] that [point of difference] because [reason to believe].*
- Internal: *To [target], [brand] is the [category we want to win] that [how we beat competitors].*

Mountain Dew example: 16–24-year-old males who want excitement; among CSDs, great taste that exhilarates, because it is energizing, thirst-quenching, and a unique citrus flavor.

Compare to Dolan’s four elements (target, wants, category, key benefit). Class adds **reason to believe** and a clean “frame of reference.”

**Positioning vs. differentiation:**

- Positioning = the picture in the customer’s mind.
- Differentiation = real or perceived differences that make that picture true. That is what lets you write the 4 Ps.

**Differentiation tools:** branding is the most effective. Otherwise: features (facts) → advantages (how it performs) → benefits (need satisfied), plus customer support. Same Gupta rule: do not stop at features.

**Reposition** when performance is weak or the competitive/context picture changes.

## 6. Simulation (slides 9–23) — Q1 this week

You run a new **3D-printed carbon fiber bicycle** line for a large international bike firm. Several rivals enter at once. Direct sales through company-owned outlets in major bike markets (North America, South America, Europe/Africa, Asia). Target is the **higher end of the conventional market** that wants the cachet of carbon fiber, fully customized, higher price than traditional high-end bikes. Customization → little inventory → smaller retail footprint → mostly DTC.

Each quarter: get the situation → set strategy and tactics → sim runs against opponents → results come back.

| Quarter | What you do |
|---|---|
| **Q1 (this week)** | Name the company, analyze the market survey, **choose the initial target segment**, schedule **one** sales outlet |
| Q2 | Design a brand, hire a few salespeople, price, small ad campaign |
| Q3 | Read feedback/competition/finance; adjust; create web page(s) |
| Q4 | Second target segment + mix; new outlet; salespeople; start social |
| Q5 | New brands/R&D features; more ads, people, outlets; paid internet ads |
| Q6 | Refine; balanced scorecard (profit, satisfaction, targeted share) |

**Q1 decisions are due Sunday at 11:45 p.m. Mountain Time** — 14 minutes earlier than the quiz, discussion, and reflection (11:59 p.m.). Decisions cannot be late.

The sim grades financial performance, marketing effectiveness, market performance, strategic thinking/tactical execution, and readiness for the future.

Q1 is a live STP exercise: do not pick a segment that is merely large; pick one you can serve, that values customization/carbon fiber, and that you can reach with a single outlet.

## Key takeaways

1. **STP is the aspiration decision** sitting on the 5 Cs. Segment first, then target, then position; the 4 Ps come after.
2. **Value is relative.** Customers compare you to brand, category, generic, and total-budget alternatives. Define competition at all four levels.
3. **Everything can be differentiated.** Compete on problem-solving, not price. Features → advantages → benefits.
4. **Segments are discovered.** Group by who / behavior / why. A segment that is not identifiable, substantial, accessible, stable, differentiable, and actionable is not a strategy.
5. **Targeting is a three-way fit:** attractive segment + you can serve it + competition leaves room. Exclude people on purpose.
6. **Positioning is a sentence the whole firm can use:** target + frame of reference + point of difference + reason to believe. If you skip it, the market writes it for you.
7. **Q1 sim:** one segment, one outlet, name the company. Decision lock is **Sunday 11:45 p.m. MT**.
