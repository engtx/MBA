Marketing departments are often considered as public facing areas of companies that attempt to encourage buying a product, at least that's what I think. As it turns out the role Marketing departments play has an almost fundamental impact on the direction of the overall company. Marketing departments offer a unique value in companies compared to other functional groups because their impact reaches both inside and outside of the organization. In a Market-Driven environment, Marketing department study and interface with the the public to establish the features lists which are then translated into deliverables to engineering and R&D teams, effecticely decided what products will be created and the ultimate fate of the company. The ability to join the two into one department offers a pipeline from concept to reality with increased likelyhood of customer adoptions. At Broadcom, we offer Business to Business products. Apple buys our parts. Broadcoms marketing department is likely studying consumer interests such as lighter, smaller, faster phones. Apple wants to keep up with these customers and those demands must be met by broadcoms product. If broadcom doesn't evaluate and plan for future needs, they can lose ground against competition



I used to think of marketing as the public-facing entity that tries to get people to buy things. After the Week 1 readings, I now find that to be a narrow view. Marketing is how a company decides which customers to create and keep, and how it turns that choice into products. That is why it shapes the whole firm, not just the ads we see.

Marketing reaches between the outside world and the inside of the company. On the outside, it studies customers, competitors, collaborators, and context (the 5 Cs). On the inside, it translates that analysis into an vision of who we serve and why they should choose us. That vision turns into action plans (product, promotion, place, price) which converts into deliverables to engineering and R&D departments. In a market-driven company marketing learns what customers value and that becomes the feature list and the roadmap. If you get that pipeline right, adoption is more likely. Get it wrong and the company builds the wrong things.

Broadcom is a great example of this. We don't sell phones, we sell a product that is used to create Apple's phones, therefor, Apple's customers set the rules. If the consumers want a lighter, smaller, faster device, Apple has to deliver that, and Broadcom has to have the silicon ready to met the demand. So Broadcom marketing is not just promoting chips, it has to understand Apple’s decision-making, Apple’s next-best alternatives (our competitors), and the consumer trends that will show up in Apple’s latest specs. If we only react to today’s purchase order, we likely would lose the next generation design win. If we anticipate the need and build for it, we keep Apple and make it harder for competitors to replace us.

The readings did change my view. Marketing creates companies. And can also detroy them.
