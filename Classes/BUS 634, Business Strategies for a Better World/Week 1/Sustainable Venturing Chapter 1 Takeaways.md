# Sustainable Venturing Chapter 1 Takeaways

Notes from Thomas J. Dean, *Sustainable Venturing: Entrepreneurial Opportunity in the Transition to a Sustainable Economy* (Pearson, 2014), **Chapter 1: Introduction to Sustainable Venturing**.

Chapter 1’s core argument: **environmental problems are economic opportunities**, and sustainable venturing is entrepreneurship aimed at those opportunities. Profit is not a side effect. It is the mechanism.

---

## The big idea

**Entrepreneurship** = creating value by combining resources in a unique way to pursue an **economic opportunity**.

**Sustainable venturing** = the same process, except the value created is **socially and ecologically sustainable** — it helps society while sustaining or improving the planet’s resources.

Dean treats *sustainable venturing*, *sustainable entrepreneurship*, and *environmental entrepreneurship* as basically the same. **Social entrepreneurship** is included because social and environmental problems are linked.

Opening examples (WhiteWave/Silk, Renewable Choice, CraftSkills East Africa) show this is already happening at many scales: high-growth cleantech, small lifestyle businesses, and local solutions in emerging markets.

---

## Why this is an opportunity, not just a problem

- Entrepreneurs get rewarded for creating value people want (Smith’s invisible hand).
- An **opportunity** is a market gap / imperfection — a **problem waiting to be solved**. Easy ones disappear fast. Real ones are windows that open and close as rewards and competition change.
- Earth is a **closed system** (except solar input). We only have one planet’s resources.
- **Ecosystem services** (air, climate, water, food, materials, recreation) were estimated at ~$33T/year — larger than global GNP in the source Dean cites.
- Humanity’s **ecological footprint** was ~**1.4 Earths**. Overuse draws down **natural capital**.
- **Sustainable development** (Brundtland): meet present needs without compromising future generations.
- If activity is not socially and ecologically sustainable, it will not stay **economically** sustainable either. Those three are linked.

Cairncross (1990): the environment may be “the biggest opportunity for enterprise and invention the industrial world has ever seen.”

---

## What counts as a sustainable venture

Not only Tesla-style cleantech. Dean’s map:

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| High-growth cleantech | Tesla, advanced batteries, Clipper Windpower |
| Small / lifestyle | Eco-Handyman, Namaste Solar |
| Non-tech but scaled | Horizon Organic, Evolution Markets, RecycOil |
| Corporate | Eaton hybrids, Toyota/Honda hybrids, GE **ecomagination** (“green is green”) |
| Process / culture, not the product itself | New Belgium (wind-powered, waste-to-energy), Patagonia |

Shared trait: new products, services, or business models that increase social/ecological sustainability.

## The Six C’s (the drivers)

Memorize this table. It is the chapter’s main framework.

1. **Costs**
   - Clean-tech costs falling (e.g., wind vs. coal)
   - Resource costs rising (fossil fuels)
   - **Internalizing externalities**: polluters start paying (carbon costs, regulation)

2. **Capital**
   - Private money: VC, PE, angels, corporates (Walmart, GE)
   - Public money: tariffs, rebates, RPS, stimulus
   - Clean energy investment cited at **$260B in 2011**

3. **Consumers**
   - **Negative screening**: avoid dirty brands
   - Some will pay a green premium (niche)
   - Many buy “green” for **non-green** reasons: efficiency, health, comfort, productivity (green buildings, organic food)

4. **Climate**
   - We already live in a **carbon-constrained world**
   - Rising **cost of carbon** changes who wins
   - Entrepreneurs can also sell carbon credits

5. **Consciousness**
   - Knowledge of climate, population, degradation is spreading
   - Beliefs eventually change the **rules of the game**
   - Usually slow; sometimes jumps after a crisis

6. **Convergence**
   - Economic incentives start lining up with good social/environmental outcomes
   - This overlap is the **locus of opportunity**
   - Best entrepreneurs **don’t wait** for the rules to change. They help **rewrite the rules** (policy, standards, markets) in their favor.

## Five myths

1. **It’s about saving the world, not profit.** Wrong. Unprofitable businesses are not sustainable and don’t scale. Proving you can make money *and* help is the point.
2. **You have to want to save the world.** Wrong. Some people just follow the profit signal. Outcome can be the same.
3. **Do good for the environment and you’ll make money.** Wrong. **Green is not always green.** Many helpful ideas are not yet good businesses. Find the intersection, or change the rules so it becomes one.
4. **Customers will prefer my green product.** Wrong, except a niche. Most buy on **performance and quality**. Sell efficiency, health, lower operating cost — the green attributes that travel with those benefits.
5. **Find the opportunity and the rest is easy.** Wrong. You still need strategy, competitive advantage, a real team, and execution.

---

## Three overlapping domains

```
Social entrepreneurship  |  Sustainable venturing  |  Corporate sustainable enterprise
```

- **Corporate sustainable enterprise:** large-firm risk, metrics, policies, systems
- **Social entrepreneurship:** often mission/impact first; roots in nonprofits
- **Sustainable venturing:** market-driven; profit and sustainability aligning

Dean’s view of **sustainability** = social + environmental + economic. You cannot get one without the others. Poverty can wreck ecosystems; degraded ecosystems worsen poverty and conflict.

## Book’s stance (why this chapter exists)

The course/book is **not** “be nicer to the planet.” It is: **use markets to capture the opportunities created by a resource-constrained planet.** Chapter 2 will turn that into systems and market failures.

If your notes already have the **definition**, the **Six C’s**, the **five myths**, and **convergence / change the rules**, you have the chapter. The company stories are illustrations, not the testable core.
