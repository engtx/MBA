# Sarasvathy What Makes Entrepreneurs Entrepreneurial Takeaways

Notes from Saras D. Sarasvathy, *What Makes Entrepreneurs Entrepreneurial?* (Darden / University of Virginia). This is the last of the four Week 2 prep readings.

Core claim: expert entrepreneurs use a distinct logic called **effectual reasoning**. They do not start with a predicted market and then hunt for the optimal path. They start with **who they are, what they know, and whom they know**, take action, and let goals emerge.

---

## The study (why this is not just war stories)

In 1997 Sarasvathy had **30 founders** (firms $200M–$6.5B, 17 states, steel to biotech) talk aloud for two hours through the **same 17-page problem set**: build a company from the **same product idea**. Analyses of the tapes produced a coherent, teachable logic: **effectuation**.

The same person can use both causal and effectual reasoning. The best entrepreneurs use both. They **prefer effectual logic in the early venture**, and many **do not transition well** into later stages that need more causal/managerial thinking.

---

## Causal vs. effectual

| | **Causal (MBA / managerial)** | **Effectual (entrepreneurial)** |
|---|---|---|
| Starts with | A **pre-determined goal** and given means | A **given set of means**; goals emerge |
| Question | What is the optimal (fastest, cheapest) path to the goal? | What effects can I create with what I have? |
| Metaphor | Genghis Khan conquering known fertile land | Columbus sailing uncharted water |
| Creativity | Optional (strategic thinking = invent *new means* for a *given* goal) | Inherent |
| Cooking analogy | You get a menu, pick recipes, shop, cook in your kitchen | No menu; strange kitchen; cook from whatever is in the cupboards |
| Extra skills | Domain skill | Plus imagination, spontaneity, risk-taking, **salesmanship** |
| Planning | Plan carefully, then execute | **Lives in execution**; plans are made/unmade daily |
| Surprises | Deviations from the path | **The path.** Flora and fauna of the jungle |

Classroom entrepreneurship is extremely causal: idea → research → projections → team → plan → financing → prototype → market → exit, with a footnote that surprises happen. Expert entrepreneurs treat surprise as **the work**.

---

## The process: three means

Every entrepreneur starts with:

1. **Who they are** — traits, tastes, abilities
2. **What they know** — education, training, expertise, experience
3. **Whom they know** — social and professional networks

They start small, with what is closest at hand, and move **almost directly into action**. Means and possible effects keep reconfiguring through interaction. Eventually some effects coalesce into real goals — landmarks in the wilderness.

**Restaurant example**

- **Causal:** industry research → location → segment → target on expected return → design → raise money → team → operate.
- **Effectual:** she is an Indian chef with little money. Bring lunch to downtown friends; some subscribe; maybe a restaurant later. **Or** they care about her culture/philosophy, and the firm becomes education, travel, retail, packaging, self-help — same person, different market.

Same starting product in the study → companies in **18 completely different industries**.

Figure 2: textbooks go market definition → segmentation → targeting (expected return) → positioning. Experts go **customer identification via the three means** → partnerships and selling → add segments/partners → **one of several possible markets**. After a market exists, you can flip to causal marketing to grow share.

## Three principles (the inversions of causal logic)

### 1. Affordable loss (not expected return)

Managers pick the segment with the highest expected return. Entrepreneurs ask: **what can I afford to lose?** Extreme form: **zero resources to market**.

Several experts said they would skip traditional market research and **try to sell before the product exists** — “hands on actual selling.” First customer is whoever is nearby (geography, network, or expertise). That keeps them from locking onto a theorized market and leaves them open to surprise about **which market they actually build**.

### 2. Strategic partnerships (not competitive analysis)

At startup, detailed competitor analysis often “does not make sense” because the market is not assumed to exist yet. One founder: ordered people **not** to think about competitors (later, at $3B, they did lots of competitive research — effectual logic gets **supplemented/replaced** as the firm grows).

Ideal start: **turn customers into partners**. Pick flagship accounts, “package yourself early” before putting a lot of capital out. Pre-commitments **cut uncertainty**. The **expanding partner network**, not a pre-chosen TAM, largely decides which market you end up in.

Partnerships + affordable loss = get to market with very little capital.

### 3. Leveraging contingencies (not prediction / exploiting pre-existing knowledge)

Heart of entrepreneurial expertise: **turn the unexpected into the profitable.** “Ready-fire-aim,” not ready-aim-aim-aim.

Great firms are **residuals of particular people** interacting with their time, place, and technology (Wedgwood + Bentley; Microsoft if IBM’s contract or Kildall’s flight had gone differently). Contingencies themselves do not create the firm. **How the entrepreneur uses them** does.

Causal reasoning tries to **avoid** surprises. Effectual reasoning treats good *and* bad surprises as **raw material**.

---

## The underlying logic

- Causal: **To the extent we can predict the future, we can control it.** Hence all the forecasting models.
- Effectual: **To the extent we can control the future, we do not need to predict it.**

**Urn metaphor**

1. Known mix of balls → risk, calculable expected value. Predict, then control.
2. Unknown mix → uncertainty, but learnable by sampling. Still causal: discover the distribution, then predict.
3. **Effectual:** the future is **not out there to be discovered**. Players **create** it. “I will put red balls in and recruit people who have red balls until almost every draw is red. If we only have green, we will invent a game where green wins.”

Several experts said a **predictable** market is a bad place to be — someone smarter with deeper pockets will predict better. An **unpredictable** market can be **shaped** with pre-committed stakeholders. Contingencies become part of the urn you are building.

Sturdy urns are **enduring human relationships** that outlive failures. Markets, in this view, are **stable configurations of stakeholders** who turn imagination into fulfilled aspirations through economic means.

Effectual logic is **people-dependent**. Causal logic is **effect-dependent** (pick the target market, then hire people who fit). Effectual: the market you create depends on **whom you can assemble**. The “right” people are not sitting on the job market; they need **emotional ownership**. You cannot always wait for them — Wedgwood: “We have to make artists of mere men.”

**Suicide quadrant** (new product × new market): traditional marketing fails; effectuation is built for this.

**Dark corollary:** if goals emerge from whoever shows up, you can also create **harmful** effects. Entrepreneurs and markets shape the species’ future, not just GDP.

## U-Haul (the worked example)

Leonard Shoen, 1945, newly married, ~$5,000. By 1949 you could rent a trailer one-way across most of the US.

Causal business plans for this usually fail: huge capital in trucks and locations, terrifying logistics, **no entry barrier** once the idea is proved.

What he actually did (effectual):

- Lived as nomads rather than buy a house (could not afford to lock up the $5k)
- Barbered part-time; bought trailers from whoever had them at the right price
- Built first trailers on the in-laws’ ranch (garage + milk house as plant)
- **Gave away** early trailers so renters would open dealerships in destination cities
- Sold trucks/trailers to employees, family, friends, investors who **leased them back** to AMERCO
- Used gas-station unused space for parking and paperwork
- Ads: Yellow Pages + orange vans on the freeway

The stakeholder network **became** the entry barrier. Failure at any moment would not have been a financial disaster because investment was **spread**.

---

## So what makes entrepreneurs entrepreneurial?

They think **effectually**. They believe in a **yet-to-be-made future** that human action can shape. If action controls the future, **prediction is not the job** — working with the people who are making it is.

**Key implication for success/failure:** effectuation may **not** raise the probability of success. It **lowers the cost of failure** by letting you fail **earlier, with less capital**.

## How this hooks to the rest of Week 2 / the course

- **Porter / Barney:** causal tools for a world where the industry and resources already exist. Effectuation is for **before** that world exists (Dean’s “opportunities as problems waiting to be solved,” often in new markets).
- **Superstars / Giants:** predictable, concentrated markets are exactly where deep pockets win. Entrepreneurs in this paper **prefer** the unpredictable so they can shape it with partners.
- **Dean Ch. 1:** sustainable venturing is not only pick an attractive sector (Porter) and lock in VRIN resources (Barney). It is also **start with means, partner, affordable loss, use surprises** — especially if you are building an impact firm against incumbents.
- **Team report:** do not treat the company as if it always ran a five-forces playbook. Ask whether it **effectuated** a market (U-Haul-style stakeholder web) and only later became causal.

If your notes already have **means → action → emerging goals**, the three principles (**affordable loss, partnerships, leverage contingencies**), **control vs. predict**, and **fail cheaper not necessarily succeed more**, you have the article.
