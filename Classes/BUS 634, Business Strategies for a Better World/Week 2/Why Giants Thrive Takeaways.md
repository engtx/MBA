# Why Giants Thrive Takeaways

Notes from *Why Giants Thrive* (“Driving forces”), The Economist special report on companies, 17 September 2016. This is the mechanism piece that follows *The Rise of the Superstars*.

Core claim: **three forces explain why giants keep winning — technology, globalisation, and regulation.** Slow growth plus disruption then lets them **buffer** shocks and use cash to buy startups and talent.

This PDF also starts the next section (“The new Methuselahs”). That is a different article; only the opening is here.

---

## The big idea

Silicon Valley used to look like a new capitalism: small, nimble, fluid, nomadic talent. It is now **corporatised**. A handful of **winner-takes-most** firms own the innovation center. Startups mostly **serve the giants or get acquired**.

The most powerful driver is **technology**. Globalisation and regulation push the same way. Together they reverse the Thatcher/Reagan “age of entrepreneurialism.”

---

## 1. Technology: scale moved from supply to demand

Industrial giants used **supply-side** economies of scale: more steel → lower unit cost → crush smaller rivals → more money for R&D, marketing, distribution.

Tech giants reinvented that for the virtual age by shifting to the **demand side (network effects)**. Success is self-reinforcing and follows **increasing returns**. Some networks even **pay people to become customers** to reach scale. Effects get stronger when networks connect into **multi-sided platforms**.

Most new tech firms are **platforms**: they sit between groups of users and enable mutually beneficial exchange. Older tech firms are pushing the platform side too. Everyone wants to be at the center of a web of users and devices that keeps opening new growth.

Scale examples in this piece:

- Google: ~4 billion searches a day
- Facebook monthly users > China’s population
- Amazon: ~**one-third of cloud computing**, cloud division growing >50% in a year
- World’s three most valuable companies were all tech; Amazon and Facebook were #6 and #7 (as of the 2016 chart)

They look less like overgrown startups and more like traditional corporations: still hoodies and open plan, but more spend on **sales, branding, legal, quality control**. Customers want **perfect** products, not “right 90% of the time.” That is why startups increasingly **sell themselves** to incumbents who can supply those functions.

## 2. Globalisation: MNCs got bigger and more complex

The biggest winners from post–mid-1990s liberalisation are large multinationals.

UNCTAD on the top 100 MNCs:

- Substantially larger (sales, employment) since the mid-1990s
- Average **20 holding companies**, often in low-tax jurisdictions
- **500+ affiliates** in **50+ countries**

How they use that size:

- Global **supply chains** with hundreds of partners (subsidiaries + contractors)
- **Chop the business into chunks** and park each chunk in the cheapest capable place
- **Alliances**: Ghemawat — America’s top 1,000 public companies get **40% of revenue from alliances**, vs **1% in 1980**
- **Knowledge networks**, not just production: Strategy& — firms that put **60%+ of R&D abroad** had higher operating margins, ROA, and faster operating-income growth
- Buy more innovation per dollar (cheaper locations) and tap local know-how. GE develops **>25% of new healthcare products in India** (frugal innovation). GE revenue outside the US: **$4.8B (1980) → $65B (2015)**

Emerging-market challengers are now part of the giant class:

- Fortune Global 500: **18 EM firms (1995) → 156** (at time of writing)
- McKinsey: **~45% of the Fortune Global 500 from emerging economies by 2025**
- Tencent WeChat → ~700m users in a few years
- Huawei: 150,000 staff, **about half in R&D**

If Western firms want to survive that, they have to get **even bigger and more innovative** — which reinforces consolidation.

## 3. Regulation: high fixed costs favor incumbents

Enron (2001) marks the end of the late-1970s deregulation era and the start of **re-regulation**. The 2008 crisis locked it in.

US examples: Sarbanes-Oxley (2002), Affordable Care Act (2010, ~1/6 of the US economy), Dodd-Frank (2010).

Enforcers got bigger and busier:

- SEC budget: **$300m (1995) → $1.6B (2016)**
- FCPA average settlement: **$72m (2005) → $157m (2014)**

**Compliance has a high fixed cost**, so it hits small firms harder.

- Crain & Crain: federal regulatory cost per employee **$10,585** (≤19 employees) vs **$7,755** (≥500 employees)
- Younger firms suffer more (less experience)
- SOX: non-exec director pay at smaller firms **$5.91 → $9.86** per $1,000 of sales
- JOBS Act (2012) carved out some relief; startups and IPOs still weak

Complexity is itself a barrier:

- Tax code **>4 million words**; Dodd-Frank **2,319 pages** (“Lawyers’ and Consultants’ Full-Employment Act”)
- GE: **900 people** in the tax division; paid **almost no tax in 2010**
- Small firms buy outside lawyers and still risk the IRS

SOX/Dodd-Frank set the tone in Britain and continental Europe. China got more zealous too (nationalist/political goals + conflict-of-interest fears). **Different regional rulebooks** make the maze worse. In many markets only the most sophisticated firms can operate.

## Bonus force: slow growth + disruption

Paradox: weak Western growth **and** technological upheaval. Big firms handle that better.

Martin Reeves (BCG): giants are good at **“buffering.”** Spare resources absorb shocks; they can move operations if politics turns against them.

- Five-year mortality, all US listed firms: **~36%**
- Same for firms worth **>$1B: about half that**

Joseph Gruber & Steven Kamin (Fed): big firms increasingly **save more than they spend**. Apple holds ~**25% of market cap in cash**. Cash piles let leaders **buy startups and hoover talent**.

## The article’s own caveat (and the next piece)

Superstars look to have every advantage. Two objections, saved for the next article:

1. The same forces that speed **creation** could speed **destruction**.
2. Superstars may just be **holdouts** against a more fluid economy.

This PDF then opens “The new Methuselahs”: Nokia went from “we will be the world’s biggest entertainment network” (2009) to selling the phone business to Microsoft. Blackberry, Blockbuster, Borders, Barings. McKinsey: average S&P 500 tenure **61 years (1958) → 18 (2011)**; **75% of the 2011 list gone by 2027**. Bain: successful newcomers reach Fortune 500 scale **more than twice as fast** as 20 years earlier. That is the *objection*, not this article’s conclusion. The next piece argues superstars are **more resilient** than those examples imply.

## How this hooks to Porter / Superstars / Dean

- **Porter:** this is *why* entry barriers and rivalry look the way they do — network effects, global scale, regulatory fixed costs, cash-funded acquisitions.
- **Rise of the Superstars:** that piece is the *what* (concentration stats). This piece is the *why* (tech / globalisation / regulation).
- **Dean Ch. 1:** if you want to build an impact venture against these giants, you either find a pocket they ignore, ride a substitute/platform shift, or **change the rules** — because the current rules (including regulation) often protect the incumbent.

If your notes already have **supply-side scale → demand-side network/platforms**, **alliances 1% → 40%**, **regulation as a fixed-cost barrier**, and **buffering + cash to buy startups**, you have this article.
