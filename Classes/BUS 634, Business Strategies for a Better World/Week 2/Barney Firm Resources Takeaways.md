# Barney Firm Resources Takeaways

Notes from Jay Barney, *Firm Resources and Sustained Competitive Advantage*, *Journal of Management* 17(1), 1991, 99–120. Syllabus: **read through page 112.** That is the definitions + VRIN framework. Applications start at the bottom of 112 and continue after the assigned cutoff.

Core argument: **sustained advantage comes from inside the firm** — heterogeneous, immobile resources that are valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable, and non-substitutable. Porter’s five forces explain which *industries* are attractive. This paper explains why *some firms inside the same industry* outperform others.

---

## The big idea (vs. Porter)

SWOT has two sides. Recent strategy research (Porter) focused on the **external** side: attractive industries, five forces. Those models quietly assume:

1. Firms in an industry (or strategic group) are **identical** in strategically relevant resources and strategies.
2. If differences appear, they vanish quickly because resources are **mobile** (you can buy them in factor markets). Porter’s recipe: analyze the environment, pick a strategy, *then* acquire the resources.

The **resource-based view (RBV)** flips both assumptions:

1. Firms may be **heterogeneous** in the resources they control.
2. Those resources may be **imperfectly mobile**, so differences can last.

If resources are perfectly homogeneous and mobile, **nobody can have a sustained advantage.** Same resources → same strategies → same results.

Two common objections fail under those assumptions:

- **First-mover advantage** still requires a unique resource (information about the opportunity). Identical firms see the same chance and move in parallel.
- **Entry / mobility barriers** only work if protected firms have resources outsiders **do not** have and **cannot buy**. Otherwise outsiders copy the strategy.

Porter’s later **value chain** starts to look inside the firm. Barney’s question: once you isolate a resource on the chain, **what must it have** to be a source of *sustained* advantage?

---

## Key definitions

**Firm resources** = all assets, capabilities, processes, attributes, information, knowledge **controlled by the firm** that let it conceive of and implement strategies that improve efficiency and effectiveness. In SWOT language: **strengths**.

Three buckets:

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Physical capital** | Technology, plant, location, raw-material access |
| **Human capital** | Training, experience, judgment, intelligence, relationships, insight of people |
| **Organizational capital** | Formal structure; planning/control/coordination systems; informal relations inside the firm and with the environment |

Not every attribute is a resource. Some block good strategy, some enable bad strategy, some do nothing. Only attributes that **improve** efficiency/effectiveness count.

**Competitive advantage:** implementing a value-creating strategy **not simultaneously implemented by any current or potential competitor**.

**Sustained competitive advantage:** that, **plus** others **cannot duplicate the benefits**.

Important footnotes:

- “Competitors” includes **potential entrants**, not just current rivals (same instinct as Porter’s threat of entry).
- **Sustained ≠ lasts a long time on the calendar.** It is an **equilibrium** idea: the advantage still exists **after rivals have stopped trying to copy it**.
- Sustained does **not** mean forever. A **Schumpeterian shock** (industry structure revolution) can make yesterday’s resource worthless or even a weakness. What sustained rules out is **being copied**, not being overtaken by a new world.

## The VRIN test (the framework)

Not every heterogeneous/immobile resource creates sustained advantage. It needs **four attributes** (later teaching often adds “organized” and calls it VRIO; this 1991 paper is **VRIN**):

1. **Valuable**
2. **Rare**
3. **Imperfectly imitable**
4. **Non-substitutable** (no strategically equivalent substitutes that are common or imitable)

These are empirical indicators of how heterogeneous and immobile the resource really is.

### 1. Valuable

It must exploit opportunities and/or neutralize threats. Rareness + inimitability + no substitutes do not matter if the attribute does not help performance. Then it is not even a “resource.”

**Complementarity with Porter:** five forces / industry analysis tells you *which* attributes are valuable in this environment. RBV then asks whether they can *sustain* advantage.

### 2. Rare

If lots of current or potential rivals have the same valuable resource (or the same **bundle** — almost every strategy needs managerial talent), they implement the same strategy → **no advantage**.

Valuable-but-common resources still matter: they create **competitive parity** and raise the odds of **survival**. They just do not create advantage.

How rare is rare enough? Absolute uniqueness is sufficient. A **small number** of firms can share a resource and still have advantage, **as long as that number is below what would produce perfect-competition dynamics**.

Valuable + rare often = **first movers / strategic innovators** — they can conceive or implement strategies others cannot.

### 3. Imperfectly imitable

Valuable + rare is only a **temporary** advantage unless others **cannot obtain** the resource. Three reasons (can combine):

**a. Unique historical conditions (path dependence)**  
Ability to get the resource depends on a place in time and space. After that moment passes, late firms cannot buy the same history.

Examples: a location that later turns out to be gold; scientists uniquely positioned for a breakthrough; a culture that formed at founding and cannot be retrofitted. Performance depends on the **path** the firm walked, not only on today’s industry structure.

**b. Causal ambiguity**  
Nobody clearly understands **which** resources cause the advantage. Imitators do not know what to copy.

For this to *sustain* advantage, **the winning firm must be just as confused as the imitators.** If insiders understand the link better, rivals can hire those managers or study the firm until the knowledge diffuses — then ambiguity is gone. Ironic test: if you can write a clean “here is why we win” memo, it may not stay a secret.

Resources are often implicit, taken for granted, interdependent. Many plausible stories, few rigorous tests → ambiguity lasts.

**c. Social complexity**  
The resource is a social phenomenon you cannot systematically engineer: managerial relationships, culture, reputation with suppliers or customers.

You may **understand** that culture creates value (little causal ambiguity) and still be **unable to build it on purpose**. That is enough for imperfect imitability.

**Physical technology by itself is usually imitable** (buy the same machines, robots, IT). Advantage, if any, comes from the **socially complex** ability to *exploit* that technology better than firms that own the same kit.

### 4. Substitutability

No **strategically equivalent** valuable resources that are themselves common or imitable. Two resources are substitutes if each can be used, separately, to implement the **same strategy**.

Two forms:

1. **Similar substitute:** you cannot clone their top team, but you can build a different team that does the same job. If such teams are common or easy to build, no sustained advantage.
2. **Different substitute:** charismatic leader vs. a formal planning system — both produce “a clear vision of the future.” If lots of firms have (or can copy) planning systems, the charismatic founder is not a sustained advantage even though *that person* is rare and inimitable.

Substitutability is a matter of **degree**. Substitutes need not be identical, only equivalent **for the strategies they enable**. If enough firms have the substitute, or can acquire it, nobody — including the original firm — gets sustained advantage.

## Figure Two (end of assigned reading)

Heterogeneity + immobility → test value, rareness, imperfect imitability (history / causal ambiguity / social complexity), substitutability → **sustained competitive advantage**.

Applications begin on p. 112. If you stopped at 112, you have the theory. If you kept going, the paper applies the test to:

- **Formal strategic planning:** valuable, but common and highly imitable (textbooks exist). Not a sustained advantage *by itself*. It may help you *spot* other resources that are.
- **Informal / emergent strategy processes:** possibly rare and socially complex; substitutability vs. formal planning is an open empirical question.
- **Computers / networks alone:** buyable physical tech → imitable. A manager–system interface that is rare and socially complex might be; a tight experienced team could be a substitute.
- **Positive reputation** (later pages): often valuable, rare, historically and socially complex — a frequent candidate, if no cheap substitute for trust.

## How this hooks to the rest of Week 2

- **Porter:** industry structure sets the *average* profit pool and tells you what is valuable. Barney: your **resources** decide whether you can capture and **keep** an above-average piece.
- **Economist Superstars / Giants:** network effects, platforms, culture, data, and regulatory know-how look like VRIN resources. Cash and scale help, but **buyable** assets (the same cloud hardware, the same machines) are not the source of *sustained* advantage. The hard-to-copy social/historical layer is.
- **Dean Ch. 1 / team report:** the project asks for “source of competitive advantage and sustainability thereof” and “core competencies (tangible and intangible).” That is this paper. Run VRIN on the company, not just a five-forces on the industry.

If your notes already have **heterogeneity + immobility**, the four tests, **sustained ≠ long calendar time**, the three reasons for inimitability (history / ambiguity / social complexity), and **machines are copyable**, you have the assigned reading.
