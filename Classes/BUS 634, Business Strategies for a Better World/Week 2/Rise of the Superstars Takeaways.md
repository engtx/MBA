# The Rise of the Superstars Takeaways

Notes from Adrian Wooldridge, *The Rise of the Superstars*, The Economist special report on companies, 17 September 2016. This file covers the opening essay plus the history box (“What goes around”) and the start of “Why Giants Thrive.” The rest of the driving-forces piece is in `Why Giants Thrive.pdf`.

Core question: **a handful of giant firms again dominate the global economy. Good or bad?**

---

## The big idea

Theodore Roosevelt’s 1910 Osawatomie speech could be given today: a few corporate giants generate huge wealth for a few people and gain political power, threatening equality of opportunity.

Today’s colossi (Apple, Google, Amazon) play the role US Steel, Standard Oil, and Sears played then. They are a mix of reinvented old stars, emerging-market newcomers, and tech firms built from “noughts and ones.” What they share: **they combine the advantages of size with the virtues of entrepreneurialism**, pull away from rivals, and build defenses — including cash piles equal to **10% of GDP in America** and **47% in Japan**.

This reverses the 1980s–90s story. Management gurus (Peter Drucker: “the Fortune 500 is over”) thought **size was dying**: AT&T broken up, SOEs privatized, startups everywhere. That fit Ronald Coase (*The Nature of the Firm*, 1937): a firm exists only when it can do something **cheaper than the market**. Now **size matters again**.

---

## The numbers (superstar effect)

- McKinsey Global Institute: **10% of public companies generate 80% of all profits**
- Firms with **>$1B revenue** ≈ **60% of global revenues** and **65% of market cap**
- M&A boom: 11,500 deals in 1990 (2% of world GDP) → ~**30,000 a year** since 2008 (~3% of GDP). Example: AB InBev / SABMiller, $107B
- US Fortune 100 share of GDP: **33% (1994) → 46% (2013)**
- Fortune 100 share of Fortune 500 revenues: **57% → 63%**
- US listed companies: **6,797 (1997) → 3,485 (2013)** (Grullon et al.)
- Median listed firm’s sales ≈ **3×** 20 years earlier
- **Profit margins rise with market concentration**
- Startups: lowest since the late 1970s; **more firms die than are born**; average firm age is up (Litan & Hathaway)
- Workers change jobs and move across states less than at any time since the 1970s

Peter Thiel: **“Competition is for losers.”** Silicon Valley shares and margins look like the robber-baron era. Google ~**69%** of search; Google + Apple = **~90%** of smartphone OS. Top five US banks: **25% of banking assets (2000) → 45%**.

Britain and South Korea look like America. Continental Europe is less concentrated (19 → 17 of the global top 100 by market cap). Still, in every OECD country they track, firms with **>250 employees** account for the largest share of value added.

---

## Why this will strengthen

1. Giants force **rivals to bulk up**.
2. Lawyers, consultancies, and other professional services must **go global** to serve them.
3. **Digitisation**: network effects + operate across borders.

James Manyika (McKinsey): today’s giants are big in a **new way**.

| | Detroit top 3 (1990) | Silicon Valley top 3 (2014) |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $250B | $247B |
| Market cap | $36B | **>$1T** |
| Employees | **1.2 million** | **137,000** |

Old giants (Walmart, Exxon) still have lots of assets and people — but even they are thinner (Exxon: 150,000 workers in the 1960s → less than half today, after merging with Mobil). New giants look “asset-light,” then start acting like old corporates: Washington insiders, armies of lobbyists, offshore cash, aggressive tax minimization. They excel at **both** the business **and** extracting as much as possible from government while paying as little tax as possible.

## History box: “What goes around”

Alfred Chandler on US business after the Civil War: **“ten years of competition and 90 years of oligopoly.”**

Pattern: scale + technology → productivity up, prices down → consolidators claim they are the **friend of the common man**. Same movie in steel, then retail and CPG (Sears, P&G, Heinz, Ford, GM). J.P. Morgan + Carnegie → US Steel, floated at **$1.4B**. Naomi Lamoreaux on 93 consolidations (1895–1904): **72** controlled ≥40% of their industry; **42** controlled ≥70%. The Senate was “the millionaires’ club.”

Backlash was real but mild and cyclical:

- Anti-corporate: 1910s, 1930s (Sherman Act, income tax, direct election of senators)
- Pro-corporate: 1920s, 1950s
- 1980s: deregulation + globalization **unpicked** corporate America
- Digital revolution looks like **another swing back to oligopoly**

**Same as then:** winner-takes-most; cut price and buy rivals; a few names keep expanding into adjacent industries (GE into everything electrical; Google into everything informational).

**Different now:** fewer assets, weaker local roots, much more **global** — so national governments have a harder time taming them.

## “Why giants thrive” (only the opening, in this PDF)

Three named drivers: **technology, globalisation, regulation**.

Silicon Valley is the exhibit. In the 1990s it looked like a new capitalism: small, nimble, fluid, nomadic talent. Today it is **corporatised**. A handful of **winner-takes-most** firms own the innovation center. Startups mostly **serve the giants or get acquired**.

Scale → dominance: Google ~4 billion searches a day; Facebook’s monthly users > China’s population. The **infrastructure of the information economy** is increasingly controlled by a handful of companies (the piece cuts off as it gets to Amazon).

## How this hooks to Porter / Dean

Porter: industry structure (five forces) sets long-run profit. Superstars are what it looks like when **entry barriers, network effects, and scale** get very high and rivalry among equals disappears.

Dean Ch. 1: sustainable venturing is about finding where incentives and impact align — often **against** entrenched giants, or by **changing the rules**. This article is the “the incumbents are pulling away” backdrop for that.
