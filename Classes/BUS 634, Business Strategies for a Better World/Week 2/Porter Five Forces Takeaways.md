# Porter Five Forces Takeaways

Notes from Michael E. Porter, *The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy*, Harvard Business Review, January 2008.

This is the 2008 update of the classic framework. Core argument: **competition for profits is broader than today’s rivals.** Industry structure — not whether a business is high-tech, growing, or “sexy” — sets medium- and long-run profitability. Strategy starts by understanding those forces, then positioning against them, exploiting shifts, or reshaping them.

---

## The big idea

The strategist’s job is to understand and cope with competition. Managers usually define competition too narrowly (only current rivals). **Extended rivalry** also includes:

1. **Threat of new entrants**
2. **Bargaining power of suppliers**
3. **Bargaining power of buyers**
4. **Threat of substitutes**
5. **Rivalry among existing competitors**

Together these five forces **define industry structure** and how the economic value created in the industry is divided: how much stays with firms vs. is bargained away by customers and suppliers, capped by substitutes, or constrained by potential entrants.

- Intense forces (airlines, textiles, hotels) → almost nobody earns attractive returns.
- Benign forces (software, soft drinks, toiletries) → many firms are profitable.

Short-run profits can be moved by weather or the business cycle. **Structure sets profitability in the medium and long run.** A healthy industry structure should matter as much as your own position.

The **strongest force (or forces)** determine profitability and should drive strategy. That force is not always obvious. Photographic film looked like a rivalry problem; the real limiter was a **substitute** (digital).

---

## 1. Threat of new entrants

New entrants bring capacity and a hunger for share. That pressures prices, costs, and required investment. Diversifying incumbents from other industries (Pepsi into water, Microsoft into browsers, Apple into music) are especially dangerous because they bring cash and capabilities.

**It is the threat of entry, not actual entry, that caps profits.** If barriers are low and retaliation looks weak, incumbents must hold prices down or spend to deter (Starbucks constantly refreshing stores/menus).

### Seven entry barriers

1. **Supply-side economies of scale** — larger volume → lower unit cost. Entrant must come in big or accept a cost disadvantage. (Intel R&D/fabs; Scotts advertising; package-delivery logistics.)
2. **Demand-side benefits of scale (network effects)** — buyer willingness to pay rises with more users. (eBay; “nobody got fired for buying IBM.”)
3. **Customer switching costs** — fixed costs of changing vendors (respec, retrain, rewrite systems). SAP ERP is the classic: embedded data, adapted processes, retraining, mission-critical apps.
4. **Capital requirements** — especially unrecoverable spend (up-front ads, R&D). Don’t overstate this: if returns look good and capital markets work, money shows up (airlines can finance planes because they have resale value).
5. **Incumbency advantages independent of size** — proprietary tech, best inputs, best locations, brand, cumulative learning. Walmart/Target bypassed malls instead of fighting department stores on their turf.
6. **Unequal access to distribution** — shelf space, travel agents. Sometimes you must bypass channels (low-cost airlines → internet booking).
7. **Restrictive government policy** — licenses, foreign-investment limits, patents, safety rules. Policy can also *lower* barriers (subsidies, open basic research).

Assess barriers **relative to who might enter** (startup vs. foreign firm vs. related-industry giant). Creative entrants find workarounds.

### Expected retaliation

Entrants stay out if they expect a long, expensive fight. Fear is higher when incumbents have hit earlier entrants hard, have cash/capacity/channel clout, will cut price to hold share (especially with high fixed costs), or when growth is slow so share must be stolen.

## 2. Supplier power

Powerful suppliers capture value by raising price, limiting quality/service, or shifting costs onto the industry. **Labor is a supplier.** Microsoft squeezed PC makers on OS price; those makers could not pass it through because buyers switch easily among PCs.

A supplier group is powerful if:

- It is **more concentrated** than the industry it sells to
- It does **not depend** on this industry for much of its revenue
- Industry firms face **switching costs**
- Products are **differentiated** (patented drugs vs. generics)
- There is **no substitute** (airline pilots)
- It can **credibly threaten forward integration**

---

## 3. Buyer power

The flip side. Powerful customers force lower prices, more quality/service, and play vendors off each other.

**Negotiating leverage** if:

- Few buyers, or each buys in large volume (especially in high-fixed-cost industries)
- Products are **standardized / undifferentiated**
- **Low switching costs**
- Buyers can **credibly threaten backward integration** (Coke/beer makers making their own packaging)

**Price sensitive** if:

- The item is a **large share** of their cost or budget (mortgages)
- They earn **low profits** / are cash-strapped
- The industry’s product **barely affects their quality**
- The product does **little** to reduce their other costs

Opposite: if the product saves or makes them a lot of money (tax accounting, well logging, investment banking), they care about quality, not price.

Same logic applies to consumers and B2B. **Intermediate customers** (retailers, distributors, assemblers) get extra power when they influence downstream purchase decisions. Producers fight that with exclusive deals, direct-to-end-user marketing, or branding components (DuPont Stainmaster carpets).

---

## 4. Threat of substitutes

A substitute does the **same job by a different means**. Videoconferencing vs. travel. Plastic vs. aluminum. Email vs. express mail. Substitution can be **indirect**: urban apartments replace suburban lawns, so lawn-care demand falls; airline websites replace travel agents, so agent software is threatened.

Easy to miss because substitutes look “different.” For a Father’s Day gift, ties and power tools compete. **Doing without, buying used, or doing it yourself** are substitutes.

High threat → **price ceiling** and often slower growth. Also caps the upside in good times (mobile as first phone in emerging markets).

Threat is high if:

- Attractive **price-performance** trade-off (Skype vs. long distance; Netflix/YouTube vs. video rental)
- **Low switching costs** (branded drug → generic)

Watch other industries: better plastics made them substitutes for auto steel. Complements can raise or lower the substitute threat (charging stations vs. alt-fuel cars; iTunes sped CD → digital).

---

## 5. Rivalry among existing competitors

Familiar forms: price cuts, new products, ads, service. Rivalry limits profit depending on **intensity** and **basis**.

**Intensity is highest when:**

- Many competitors, or they are **roughly equal** (no leader to enforce discipline)
- **Slow growth** → share fights
- **High exit barriers** (specialized assets, management ego) keep zombies in, dumping capacity
- Rivals are **highly committed** beyond economics (state-owned firms, prestige, “full line,” personality clashes)
- Firms **cannot read each other’s signals** (unfamiliar, different goals)

**Price rivalry is the most destructive** — it transfers profit straight to customers, is easy to match, and trains buyers to ignore features.

Price wars are likely when products are nearly identical, switching costs are low, **fixed costs are high and marginal costs are low**, capacity must be added in big lumps, or the product is **perishable** (tomatoes, obsolete PCs, empty hotel rooms).

**Non-price rivalry** (features, service, delivery, brand) is less likely to wreck profits; it can raise value vs. substitutes and raise entry barriers.

- **Zero-sum:** everyone competes on the same dimensions / same customers.
- **Positive-sum:** rivals serve **different segments** with different mixes. Can raise average industry profit and grow the market (U.S. casinos expanding into new niches rather than only cutting payouts).

## Factors, not forces (common mistakes)

Do **not** treat these as extra forces. Trace them through the five:

| People treat this as a force | Porter’s point |
|---|---|
| **Fast industry growth** | Mutes rivalry, but can empower suppliers and attract entrants. PCs grew fast and were poorly profitable. Growth-chasing is a major source of bad strategy. |
| **Technology / innovation** | Not automatically attractive. “Mundane” industries with price-insensitive buyers, switching costs, or scale barriers often earn more than sexy tech. |
| **Government** | Not a sixth force. Patents raise entry barriers; pro-union rules raise supplier power; bankruptcy rules that prevent exit intensify rivalry. Analyze **specific policies** through the five forces. |
| **Complements** | Products used together (hardware + software; cars + gas stations). Affect demand, but not inherently good or bad for profit. They change barriers, substitutes, rivalry, buyer/supplier power. (iTunes accelerated substitution away from CDs; JVC got studios to back its VCR standard.) |

## How to do the analysis

- Time horizon = **full business cycle** (usually 3–5 years; a decade in mining). Use **average** profitability, not one year.
- Goal is **not** “attractive vs. unattractive.” Goal is **why** profits are what they are.
- **Quantify** where you can: product as % of buyer cost; share of industry sales needed for efficient scale; switching cost in dollars.
- Tie forces to the **P&L and balance sheet**: rivalry → prices/marketing/R&D; suppliers → input cost; buyers → price or extra inventory/financing; entry/substitutes → sustainable price ceiling.
- Think **systemically**: which force is constraining profit today? If one force shifts, what happens to the others?

### Typical steps

1. Define the relevant industry (product scope + geographic scope).
2. Identify and segment buyers, suppliers, rivals, substitutes, potential entrants.
3. Assess the **drivers** of each force — which are strong, which are weak, and why.
4. Test consistency: does the story match long-run profits? Are the most profitable players better positioned vs. the forces?
5. Analyze recent and likely future changes.
6. Identify structure you (or rivals/entrants) could influence.

### Defining the industry

Too broad hides real differences. Too narrow misses linkages. Use the five forces as the test: **same structure → same industry; markedly different structure → separate industries** (and a separate strategy). Example: automotive motor oil vs. truck/stationary-engine oil is chemically similar but a different industry (buyers, channels, packaging, ads, logistics).

Geographic rule is the same: similar structure everywhere → global industry; different structures → regional industries.

If you draw the boundary wrong, a careful five-forces write-up still surfaces the miss (omitted product shows up as a substitute; omitted rival shows up as a potential entrant).

## Implications for strategy (the “so what”)

Once you know *why* the industry earns what it earns, you can:

1. **Position** where the forces are weakest, and tailor the value chain to that pocket.
2. **Anticipate / exploit structural change.**
3. **Reshape** the forces so the industry is more favorable.

Best strategies do more than one.

### Positioning — Paccar

Heavy trucks: powerful fleet buyers, standardized products, capital-intensive cyclical rivalry, strong unions, rail as a substitute for trucking services. Paccar targets **owner-operators** (less clout, less price-sensitive, emotional attachment), customizes Kenworth/Peterbilt, supports uptime, and gets a ~10% price premium. 68 years profitable, long-run ROE above 20%. They found the **weaker-force segment** and built the whole chain around it.

Also use the framework for **entry and exit**. Exit if structure is poor/declining and you have no superior position. Enter if you can see a good future **before** it is in the acquisition price, or if you can beat barriers cheaper than others.

### Exploiting change — music

Digital looked like it would destroy labels by removing physical distribution as a barrier. A structural read said the real barriers were **risk-pooling across many artists** and **promotional clout with radio/retail**. Majors stayed; new labels stayed rare. What *did* change: illegal downloads as a substitute, then Apple/iTunes as a new gatekeeper because labels would not build a shared platform. Structure shifted against them; majors went from six to four.

### Shaping structure

Two paths:

- **Redivide the pie** — keep more of existing value away from suppliers, buyers, substitutes, and entrants (standardize parts, add vendors, raise buyer switching costs, differentiate, escalate R&D/marketing, improve availability vs. substitutes). **Sysco:** private-label brands (cut supplier power), value-added services (less pure price), IT and regional DCs (higher entry barriers). Industry consolidated; profits rose.
- **Expand the pie** — grow demand, raise quality, cut inherent industry cost, serve latent buyers, clean up channels, collaborate with suppliers. Soft-drink bottler rationalization helped both concentrate and bottlers. Expanding the pie does **not** repeal the five forces — they still decide who gets the extra value. Winners expand the pool **in ways they capture disproportionately**.

Industry leaders have a special job here: improving structure is a public good, and the leader usually captures the most. Sometimes that is more profitable than grabbing more share (which triggers rivals, customers, suppliers).

**Dark side:** you can wreck structure while “winning.” IBM’s open PC architecture attracted complements, but handed OS and chips to Microsoft and Intel, standardized the product, and created enduring price rivalry and supplier power.

Merging to “eliminate rivals” is risky. A profit windfall attracts **new** entrants and backlash from buyers/suppliers (Manhattan banking consolidations, then a new wave of banks).

## Common pitfalls

- Defining the industry too broadly or too narrowly
- Making lists instead of analyzing
- Treating all five forces equally instead of digging into the ones that actually control profit
- Confusing **effect** (price sensitivity) with **cause** (buyer economics)
- Static snapshot; ignoring trends
- Mixing cyclical/transient noise with structural change
- Using the framework only to stamp “attractive / unattractive” instead of **guiding choices**
