# Module 1 Reading Takeaways

Notes from Josh Peterson, *From Good Ideas to Great Results: How to Speak Business and Make an Impact* (2025). Required pre-reading for BUS 500 Module 1 (Aug 27).

Josh’s through-line: **the best idea doesn’t usually win. The best-communicated idea that makes clear business sense does.** Technical skill is not enough. You advance by pairing **clear structure** with **financial logic**.

---

## The integration imperative

Two skills, not two courses:

- **Clear, structured communication** — so ideas are understood, remembered, and acted on. Not elegant prose.
- **Financial reasoning** — so you can say how the idea creates value, in the language executives use.

They amplify each other. Finance is the logic that makes communication persuasive. Communication is what turns sound logic into a decision.

Two proof cases:

- **Equifax:** a known Apache Struts patch sat unused because the right people never got the alert. Communication failure turned a fixable bug into a **~$4B** disaster.
- **Netflix Culture Deck:** Hastings/McCord led with a concrete philosophy (“team, not a family”; “adequate performance gets a generous severance”), not vague values. Clear communication attracted the people they needed.

Three failure modes he keeps returning to: the marketer who can’t turn “brand awareness” into money, the analyst who buries the insight on slide 23, the salesperson who hits quota but can’t talk margins.

## Section II — Think in SCQA, speak in a pyramid

**Barbara Minto’s Pyramid Principle** (McKinsey). Think in this order:

1. **Situation** — context
2. **Complication** — why anyone should care
3. **Question** — the decision
4. **Answer** — the recommendation

**Communicate in reverse:** lead with the answer. Busy people need the bottom line first, then details if they want them. Academic “build to the conclusion” writing fails here.

Kahneman’s reason: **System 1** scans for the point. If it can’t find it, people stop or misunderstand. Mystery-novel structure creates suspense in fiction and **impatience in business**.

Supporting points must be *reasons the recommendation is right*, not a feature list.

Mistakes to avoid:

- **Data dump** — twenty facts instead of the three that matter for this decision
- **Chronological trap** — “first we analyzed…” instead of the argument
- **Feature list** — what it does vs. why it matters
- **Buried conclusion** — saving the ask for the end

Practice: rewrite the first paragraph of your last report so sentence one is the ask.

## Section III — Make it stick: SUCCESS

Chip and Dan Heath. Structure gets you understood; SUCCESS gets you remembered.

| Letter | Meaning | Business version |
|---|---|---|
| **S**imple | Core only | One actionable recommendation, sticky-note test |
| **U**nexpected | Break the pattern | Dropbox: not “cloud storage,” “files don’t live on devices” |
| **C**oncrete | Visual, specific | “7 checkout steps ? 3, 4 min ? 90 sec,” not “improve CX” |
| **C**redible | Trust | Data, internal proof, **and** honest ranges/assumptions |
| **E**motional | Why they should care | Human impact, not just +20% efficiency |
| **S**tories | Situation ? conflict ? resolution | Let people mentally rehearse the change |

Use them **with** SCQA: concrete situation, unexpected complication, simple question, credible + emotional answer, stories in the support.

Don’t force all six. Don’t overdo emotion. Simple ? incomplete. Stories must prove the point, not decorate it.

## Section IV — Everyday writing is your brand

Most people’s first read of your competence is an email, not a meeting.

**Subject lines** are a mini-conclusion: *what is this, what do you need from me?*
“Meeting” / “Question” lose. “Q3 Launch: Need decision on budget by Friday” wins.

**Email SCQA:** request first ? just enough context ? details for the decision ? next step and when. End with “please approve by Friday,” not “let me know your thoughts.”

Other rules: match stakes to tone; casual ? sloppy; if it scrolls, it may be a doc or a meeting; fewer CCs; don’t reply-all; never attach without saying what’s in it and why.

**Reports:** the exec summary *is* the answer, complete enough to stand alone. Headers should be conclusions (“Small business churn 34% above industry”), not topics (“Customer Data Analysis”).

Slack/Teams: threads, spare @mentions, extra explicit because async. Proofread — one typo in a high-stakes note taxes your credibility. Plan with SCQA before typing.

## Section V — AI as partner, not substitute

People who use AI well already know these principles. Treat it like a calculator: fast at mechanics, useless at choosing the right problem.

**AI is good at:** brainstorming, structure, tone/clarity, grammar, first drafts from *your* outline.
**You stay essential for:** strategy, culture/politics, empathy, relationships, accountability for accuracy.

Process: **think first** (objective, audience, SCQA, 3–4 key points) **then** prompt. Prompts need audience, ask, numbers, and tone. Iterate. Rewrite so it sounds like you.

**Don’t put in AI:** secrets, HR, legal, customer PII. Don’t let it make the actual budget/strategy/crisis call. Don’t use it for real networking or mentoring.

You own the output: fact-check, logic, audience fit, authenticity. Follow company disclosure rules. Use AI to sharpen SCQA and SUCCESS, not to skip them.

This matches the syllabus: tutor/study partner/proofreader is fine; copy-paste is plagiarism; quizzes/exams are off-limits.

## Section VI — Finance is the language of every job

Every role is a financial role. The question behind every decision: **will this create more value than it costs?**

The hardware engineer’s “thermal management optimization” died until it became: **120 engineering hours ? failure rate 8% to 3% ? $180k warranty savings + $240k lost-sales avoided.** Same idea, fundable.

Decision-makers need five things in money terms:

1. What problem, and what is it costing us?
2. What will the solution cost (cash, time, opportunity)?
3. What results, and how confident are we?
4. What are the risks, and how do we mitigate them?
5. Why this vs. other uses of the same resources?

Know **how the company makes money**: revenue story (who pays, which products, why us), **COGS** vs **operating expenses**, margins, growth, competitors, and how *your* work moves those numbers.

Translate function-speak into CAC, LTV, conversion, churn, capacity, warranty, retained revenue. Start with 2–3 metrics for your own job and how they hit the P&L.

## Section VII — How it all stacks (Sarah)

Operations manager, CRM company. Onboarding = 45 days.

- **SCQA:** 45-day onboard ? 15% refunds, 40% lower CSAT, CS team stuck in setup ? should we redesign? ? **yes, 10 days**.
- **SUCCESS:** Amazon-wouldn’t-wait-six-weeks framing, real customer story, benchmarks.
- **AI:** research and polish only; she kept the judgment.
- **Finance:** problem ? **$1.7M** lost revenue; invest **$225k + $146k/year**; expect **~$1.6M** annual benefit; **~2-month payback**; still <6 months if benefits are half.

She led the room with the money sentence. Approved. Later: 12-day onboard, 6% refunds, promotion.

Daily checklist before anything important: What’s the recommendation (SCQA)? Will they remember it (SUCCESS)? What’s the financial case? Where can AI help without replacing my judgment?

**This week:** one real communication in SCQA. **This month:** one business case with numbers.
